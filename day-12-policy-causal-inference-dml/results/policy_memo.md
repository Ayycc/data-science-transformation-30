# Policy memo: did the job-training programme work?

**Important:** this memo is written from a SYNTHETIC simulation built to test the analysis method. The numbers show what the method finds when the true answer is known; they are not findings about a real programme.

## Bottom line

- **The programme raises earnings.** Enrolling raises next-year earnings by about **$2,243 a year** on average (95% range $2,111 to $2,375).
- **It raises employment.** The chance of being employed rises by about **12.9 pp** (95% range 11.4 pp to 14.4 pp).
- **The simple comparison gets the answer badly wrong.** Comparing enrolled with non-enrolled people suggests enrolment *lowers* earnings by $2,818, because the people who enrol were already earning less. That comparison is off by $5,101.
- **County rollout data agree.** Counties that started the programme saw employment rates rise by about 1.35 percentage points over the quarters after launch (95% range 1.11 to 1.58). The effect grows the longer a county has run the programme.

## Who benefits most

- People unemployed when they enrol gain about $3,488 a year, against $1,835 for people who already had a job.
- By age: about $2,616 for under-30s, $2,369 for 30-44s and $1,718 for people aged 45 and over.
- By schooling: about $2,465 for people with 11 or fewer years of school, against $1,937 for people with 14 or more.
- If places are limited, offering them to the 30% of people with the highest predicted gain would deliver about $3,502 per participant, against $2,283 if places were filled at random.

## How sure are we?

- The result passed all 7 stress tests. When enrolment was replaced with random labels, the estimated effect disappeared. Adding a made-up extra factor, or re-running on 80% of the data, barely moved it. The method also found no 'effect' on earnings from *before* the programme, which is what we would expect if it is working properly.
- In this simulation the employment range narrowly missed the true effect (11.2 pp): the estimate was 1.7 pp too high. When the whole study was repeated on 10 fresh simulated populations, the range contained the truth 100% of the time for employment and 100% for earnings. A 95% range is expected to miss about 1 time in 20.
- The main risk is a factor we cannot see, such as motivation, that drives both enrolment and earnings. That factor would have to be very strong to explain the result away: it would need to raise both the chance of enrolling and the chance of being employed by a factor of 1.80 (the E-value).
- Before launch, early-adopting counties were on employment trends that were not statistically different from those of counties that never adopted (p = 0.10). That supports the county comparison.

## Recommendation

1. **Scale the programme**, starting with counties that have high unemployment.
2. **Give enrolment priority** to people who are unemployed, younger, or have less formal education.
3. **Roll out in stages** across counties and keep some later-start counties as a comparison group, so the effect can be measured again with real data.
4. **Collect a motivation or job-readiness score at intake.** It is the most likely hidden factor, and recording it would remove the main remaining doubt.
